# Writing Style Guide

This guide is based on the current article library, especially essays such as:

- `agile-development-a-pattern-for-improvement`
- `failure-is-an-option`
- `improve-focus-actionable-ways`
- `build-showcase-skills-without-degree`
- `be-curious-not-opinionated`
- `write-and-live-with-more-meaning`
- `asking-useful-questions`

## Core Voice

The voice is:

- thoughtful
- direct
- earnest
- practical
- intellectually curious
- optimistic without sounding self-help-y or inflated

The writing sounds like a reflective software engineer explaining useful ideas to capable adults, not like a guru, marketer, therapist, or academic.

## Core Thesis Of The Site

Most essays orbit the same belief:

Use iteration, feedback, systems, experimentation, and humility to build a more intentional and fulfilling life.

New writing should usually reinforce or intelligently extend that worldview.

## Typical Article Shape

A common article structure is:

1. Open with a concrete story, person, quote, or example.
2. Extract a general principle from that example.
3. Break the principle into a few clear sections or steps.
4. Translate the lesson into everyday life, work, or creative practice.
5. End with a concise takeaway or imperative line.

Not every article uses all five steps, but most of them rhyme with this pattern.

## Sentence-Level Style

Prefer:

- short to medium sentences
- plain English over abstract jargon
- one idea per sentence when possible
- concrete nouns and verbs
- purposeful repetition for emphasis

Use rhetorical questions sparingly but naturally. They are common in introductions and transitions when framing a dilemma.

## Paragraph Style

- Keep paragraphs fairly short.
- Use single-paragraph sections for emphasis when needed.
- Let major claims land cleanly before moving to the next idea.
- Do not bury the main lesson under long setup.

## Common Rhetorical Moves

- Start with a story from software, music, sports, books, or biography.
- Move from the specific example to a broader life principle.
- Name the principle clearly.
- Break it into numbered or titled sections.
- Tie abstract advice back to behavior people can actually change.
- End with a compact, memorable line.

## Tone Rules

Do:

- sound like a smart, grounded human being
- make claims confidently when they are practical and defensible
- invite reflection without becoming vague
- use encouragement through clarity, not hype
- keep the reader oriented toward action and understanding

Do not:

- sound salesy
- sound therapeutic or confessional
- overuse internet slang
- write like an SEO farm
- stack buzzwords
- overstate certainty on complex psychological claims

## Subject Matter Preferences

Recurring source domains:

- software engineering
- productivity systems
- books and authors
- creativity
- career development
- failure and feedback
- focus and attention
- intentional living

New articles fit best when they connect one of these domains to a usable life principle.

## Structural Preferences

Headings often work best when they are:

- short
- explicit
- principle-driven

Examples of good heading patterns for this site:

- `How to Fail Well`
- `Credentialism`
- `Agile Is a Life Skill`
- `Set Yourself Up for Success`

## Endings

The site favors endings that:

- restate the principle in cleaner form
- compress the lesson into a memorable line
- call for action without sounding like a CTA button

Examples of ending energy:

- "Learn to fail well."
- "Be curious, not opinionated."
- "Build your skills by building artifacts."

## Use Of Examples

Examples are one of the site's strongest habits. Good examples usually come from:

- a recognizable person
- a book
- a business or engineering case
- the author's own observation or experience

The example should not be trivia. It should do real argumentative work.

## Writing For This Site

If you are drafting a new article, aim for this checklist:

- Lead with a concrete hook.
- State the lesson early.
- Make the article about one core idea, not five.
- Use 2 to 5 section breaks if the piece is long.
- Include at least one practical translation from theory to behavior.
- End on a distilled takeaway.

## Meta Copy Guidance

Titles:

- clear and searchable
- often principle-first
- often framed as a lesson, question, or direct claim

Descriptions:

- 1 to 2 sentences
- explicitly name the problem and promise the lesson
- avoid empty marketing language

Summaries:

- slightly more conversational than descriptions
- good place to restate the arc of the argument

## Style To Preserve During Edits

- Keep the author's calm conviction.
- Preserve simplicity over flourish.
- Do not "upgrade" the prose into something more academic, more poetic, or more generic.
- If cutting, remove redundancy before removing clarity.
- If expanding, add sharper examples or applications rather than more abstraction.
